The Counting Board

Reckoned in pebbles on a Roman counting board and copied out by a scribe, one letter at a time.

Before there were engines there were boards. A Roman who needed a sum ruled lines on a table or a slab and moved pebbles across them. The Latin for pebble is calculus, and the person moving them was a calculator.

How the board holds a number

The lines count ones, tens and hundreds. The spaces between them count fives, fifties and five hundreds. A line holds up to four pebbles and a space holds one, so the board spells a number the way a Roman wrote it. Three pebbles on the C line, one in the L space and two on the I line make CCCLII, or 352.

Adding and taking away

  1. To add, set down the pebbles of the new number. Five on a line become one in the space above, and two in a space become one on the next line.
  2. To take away, lift the pebbles of the number. When a place is empty, break a pebble from the place above into two fives or five ones.
  3. To read, call out the pebbles from the top down.

How this page is reckoned

Each letter of this page has a number, its character code, and a wax tablet lists how far each code lies from the one before. The reckoner works down the tablet, adding or taking away each figure, and reads the board aloud after every line. The scribe writes down the letter with that number.

Roman numerals have no zero. When a letter repeats, the tablet says nihil, nothing, and the reckoner reads the board again without touching it.
